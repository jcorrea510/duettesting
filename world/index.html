Under the hood

Every pixel is a question answered from scratch.

There is no 3D model of this landscape anywhere. No textures, no photos, no game engine, no libraries. For every pixel on your screen, every frame, a small program running on your GPU asks: "if I fire a ray of light from the camera through this pixel, what does it hit, and what color is it?"

The ground: noise with memory

The terrain is a height function h(x, z) built from 11 layers of gradient-aware value noise. Each layer also computes its own analytic derivatives, and steeper slopes suppress the finer layers above them. That one trick fakes millions of years of erosion: smooth valleys, sharp ridges, and the branching drainage you see in real mountains. A second, very slow noise decides where continents, islands, lagoons and open sea go.

Finding the surface: ray marching

The renderer can't "draw" a heightfield, so it walks each ray forward, step by step, checking how far above the ground it still is and taking larger strides when it's safe. Distant terrain is sampled with fewer noise layers (a level-of-detail scheme) so the horizon stays cheap.

The sky is real physics

Sky colors aren't hand-picked. A separate pass simulates sunlight scattering through a 60 km atmosphere around a 6,360 km planet: Rayleigh scattering (why the sky is blue and sunsets are red) and Mie scattering (the haze and glow around the sun). It integrates the light along each view ray and the shadowed path back to the sun, then bakes the result into a small lookup table every frame. The same table tints distant mountains with aerial perspective.

Light and shadow

  • Soft shadows: a second ray marches from each point toward the sun; how closely it grazes the terrain sets the penumbra.
  • Cloud shadows: each point looks up through the drifting cloud layer, so shadows sweep across the hills.
  • Ambient occlusion, sky light and bounce light from the ground.
  • Materials come from slope, altitude and more noise: beaches, grass, forest, rock strata, and snow that only sticks to flat high ground.

Water

The ocean knows how deep it is at every point, because the terrain function is evaluated underneath it. Light is absorbed per color channel by depth (red goes first), which is why shallow lagoons glow turquoise and deep water turns navy. Reflections use the Fresnel equations, the islands reflect into the sea with a second ray march, and foam gathers where waves touch the shore.

Keeping it smooth

The page measures its own frame rate and continuously adjusts internal resolution to hold it steady, from phones to desktops. The camera's autopilot runs the same terrain function in JavaScript to read the ground ahead, climb over ridges, and drift toward coastlines and mountains. The 4K photo button renders the frame again at higher quality in tiles, so even a phone can produce a full wallpaper.
